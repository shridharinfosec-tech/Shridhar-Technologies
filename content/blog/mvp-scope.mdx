Most MVPs don't fail because they were built badly. They fail because they were scoped badly — as a smaller version of the eventual product, rather than as a test of the one assumption that could actually kill the idea.

That distinction sounds subtle. In practice it changes almost every decision you make in the first six weeks of a new product.

## Find the assumption that would kill the project

Before scoping anything, write down the single assumption that, if wrong, means the project shouldn't continue. Not "will users like the design" — that's rarely the assumption that matters most early on. Usually it's something closer to: will a specific type of customer pay for this, will this workflow actually save them time compared to what they do today, or will a particular integration even be technically possible within your constraints.

If you can't name that assumption clearly, you're not ready to scope an MVP yet — you're still doing discovery, and that's fine. Just don't skip straight to a build.

## Cut everything that doesn't test it

Once you know the assumption, the scoping exercise becomes mechanical: for every feature under consideration, ask whether it's required to test the assumption, or whether it's something you're adding because the eventual product will need it. Eventual-product features get parked, not built.

This is where most MVP scopes quietly balloon. A founder wants to test whether small logistics companies will pay for a dispatch tool, and the scope somehow grows to include multi-user permissions, a billing system, and a mobile app — none of which are needed to find out if a dispatcher will actually use the thing daily. Every one of those additions pushes the actual test further into the future.

## A real MVP is still a real product

Cutting scope aggressively doesn't mean shipping something broken. The parts you do build need to work properly, because a prototype that falls over under real usage doesn't give you a valid answer — it just teaches you that the prototype was flimsy, which you already knew.

The practical middle ground: pick a stack and architecture that's fast to build in but won't need to be thrown away if the assumption validates. A rushed proof of concept in a throwaway stack is fine for testing feasibility internally; it's the wrong choice the moment real customers are going to depend on it, even a handful of them.

## Decide upfront what "validated" looks like

Teams that scope MVPs well also define, before building anything, what result would count as validation and what would count as a clear no. Vague success criteria ("see how it goes") tend to produce vague results, which then get argued about for weeks instead of acted on.

A better version: "if 3 of the 5 pilot customers are still using this weekly after four weeks without prompting, we build the next phase." That's specific enough to actually act on regardless of how the outcome feels emotionally in the moment.

## Plan for both outcomes

Before you start building, decide what happens if the assumption is validated and what happens if it isn't. Teams that only plan for success tend to keep building past a clear "no," because there's no pre-agreed alternative to switch to. Having that decided in advance — even loosely — makes it much easier to act on the data once it exists, in either direction.
