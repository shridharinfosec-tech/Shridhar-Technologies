There's a wide gap between "an agent that can technically call an API in a loop" and "an agent you'd trust to run unsupervised against a production system." Most of the interesting engineering work right now lives in that gap, not in the model capability itself.

Here's how we think about where agents genuinely fit today, and where they don't yet.

## Good fit: bounded, reversible, well-defined tasks

Agents do well on tasks with a clear start and end state, where the actions available are limited and well understood, and where a mistake is cheap to notice and undo. Think: pulling data from three internal systems to assemble a report, triaging incoming support tickets against a defined set of categories, or running a multi-step data cleanup job against a staging copy of a database.

These tasks share a property that matters more than people usually notice: even if the agent makes a wrong call partway through, the blast radius is small and recoverable.

## Weak fit (for now): irreversible, high-stakes, ambiguous tasks

Agents are not yet a good default for tasks where a mistake is expensive, hard to detect, or hard to undo - issuing refunds, modifying production infrastructure, or making judgment calls with legal or financial consequences. This isn't a permanent verdict on the technology; it's a statement about where the engineering discipline around guardrails, evaluation, and human oversight has actually matured enough to trust today.

Teams that skip this distinction and deploy agents against consequential systems without those guardrails are the ones generating the AI-agent horror stories that show up in the news.

## The guardrails that actually matter in practice

Three things separate a production-ready agent from a demo, more than model choice does:

1. **Scoped tool access.** An agent should only be able to call the specific functions or APIs it needs for its job, with parameters validated the same way you'd validate any external input. "The agent has access to our whole internal API" is a design smell, not a feature.
2. **Human-in-the-loop checkpoints for anything consequential.** Not every step needs a human, but any step that's expensive to undo should pause for one - even if that adds latency. Speed isn't the point of an agent that's wrong.
3. **Structured, reviewable logs.** When something goes wrong (and eventually something will), you need to be able to answer exactly what the agent did and why, not just that it "seemed to malfunction." Treat agent actions like you'd treat any other audit trail.

## Start narrower than feels necessary

The teams getting real value from agents right now tend to have started with a task most people would consider almost too boring to bother automating - and then expanded scope only after the guardrails proved themselves in production. The teams generating cautionary tales tend to have started ambitious and added safety measures after something went wrong.

That ordering matters more than most of the architecture decisions that get debated upfront.

## It's an engineering problem, not just a prompting problem

A lot of the public conversation about agents focuses on prompt design, as if the main challenge is convincing the model to behave. In our experience, the harder and more valuable work is the systems engineering around the model: what it's allowed to touch, what gets logged, what requires a human, and what happens when a multi-step task fails partway through. Get that right and the choice of underlying model becomes a much smaller decision than it initially seems.
